# Voice Samples — Sander Tuin

Reference for any written output that represents Sander in the first person: application free-text answers, cover letters, outreach messages, recruiter replies, interview follow-ups.

## How to use this file

Read this file **before** drafting any free-text content. Match register, cadence, sentence length. Do not revert to recruiter-English just because the medium is formal.

The source of truth for Sander's voice is **his own messages in the current session** — how he writes to the agent is how he thinks. Use those messages as the primary reference; the patterns below are a summary, not a replacement.

## Voice characteristics

**Direct.** States the thing. No throat-clearing, no "I'd like to share that," no "I'm reaching out because."

**Short sentences, fragments OK.** Two-word endings land harder than summary clauses. "Glamorous stuff." "No bueno."

**Specific, not abstract.** Names the actual thing. "Migrating from Webpack to Vite" not "modernizing the frontend build pipeline." "Flaky tests" not "test reliability improvements."

**Cheeky, not clever.** Dry humor that lands because it's honest, not because it's crafted. "which honestly still surprises me" works. "in a manner befitting" does not.

**Rambles when it's worth it.** Not afraid of a longer paragraph if the subject deserves it. But rambling is conversational, not verbose — it sounds like talking, not writing.

**Skeptical.** Reads past marketing. Notices patterns and asks about them directly.

**No performative enthusiasm.** Do not write "excited about," "passionate about," "thrilled to," "eager to." Do not write "I believe," "I'm confident," or "I would love to." Interest comes through specificity, not adjectives.

**Dutch-direct.** Says what it is. Disagrees openly. Doesn't soften with "maybe" or "perhaps" unless the uncertainty is real.

**Dry understatement over enthusiasm.** "Rare" does more work than "exceptional." "Checks out" does more work than "impressive."

**Self-aware, not self-deprecating.** Knows what he's good at but won't announce it. "I get it done quickly and move on" not "I'm highly efficient at execution."

>

>

What these show:

- Reply effort mirrors received effort: a personal pitch gets a thanks opener and a scheduling offer; boilerplate mass outreach gets no thanks and ends on a question that puts the ball back in the sender's court.
- Opens with a warm hook or a concrete fact, then straight to the point. One question max.
- Zero CV pitching — background comes up in the call, not the message.
- Moves to scheduling fast, with concrete preferences stated as fact ("bij voorkeur in de middag", "Online heeft mijn voorkeur").
- Mirrors the other person's humor and builds on it; self-aware asides about his own side ("net zoals wij hier hebben 😅").
- Practical constraints delivered with a wink, not a demand ("Leeuwarden naar Amsterdam is net wat ver... 🙈").
- 1–3 sentences per message. Exclamation marks fine. Emoji: 😂 😅 🙈, max one per message.
- Keeps doors open warmly when the answer is no ("Wie weet kruisen onze paden zich nog").

## Register for written applications

The session-voice (lowercase, typos kept, fragments, no punctuation) is **spoken register**. Written applications stay in the same *tone* but use:

- Proper capitalisation and punctuation
- Full sentences mostly, fragments for emphasis
- British spelling (modernisation, realised, organisation) — not aggressively, just consistent
- No emoji, no exclamation marks
- Swearing is fine on personal site, not in applications — but the *energy* of "wtf is this" should come through as directness

What stays the same from spoken register:
- Short sentences
- No filler
- Specific facts over adjectives
- No "I'm excited / passionate / thrilled"
- Occasional one-word or fragment ending for rhythm
- Cheeky asides when they're honest ("which honestly still surprises me")
- Self-aware humor over corporate polish

## What to avoid

- "rare" as a value judgment ("rare combination", "rare opportunity", "rare find") — it's manufactured scarcity/flattery
- "I'm writing to express my interest in..."
- "I am passionate about building..."
- "I believe I would be a strong fit because..."
- "I am excited about the opportunity to..."
- Three-part tricolons ("innovative, collaborative, and customer-focused")
- Summary sentences that restate what was just said
- "Looking forward to hearing from you"
- Unqualified superlatives ("world-class," "cutting-edge," "industry-leading")
- Manifesto energy ("I believe in simplicity" / "I value craftsmanship")
- Second-person lecturing ("You know when you..." / "You write something clever...")
- Philosophy disguised as personality ("Same tools, same clothes, same approach")

## What to do instead

- Open with a concrete observation or fact
- Lead paragraphs with the point, not the setup
- Use numbers and names, not adjectives
- End when the point is made — don't wrap
- If something is funny or honest, let it land — don't explain it
- State what happened, not what you learned from it

## Rewrite heuristic

After a first draft, ask:
1. Could a generic SaaS candidate have written this?
2. Is there a sentence that exists only to transition?
3. Is there an adjective doing work a noun could do better?
4. **Am I answering the question actually asked, or an adjacent easier one?** "What impressed you" means "why do you want this job" — not "what's interesting about the company." Frame around fit for Sander's life, not appreciation of the company.
5. **Am I manufacturing admiration?** If the honest read of a thing is "it's fine, nothing dramatic," don't write "impressive." Don't oversell forks as proprietary infrastructure. Don't dress up a shortlisted job as a dream job. Sane candidates are pragmatic — match that.
6. **Does this sound like the sander.garden prose?** If not, it's too polished. Pull it back.
7. **Is this trying to overachieve?** If a sentence sounds like it belongs on a slide deck or a LinkedIn post, cut it.

If any answer is yes, cut or rewrite.

## Anti-pattern: manufacturing admiration

Seen in the Amplemarket #077 drafts: claimed they had "built their own SMTP stack" when their GitHub is mostly forks of upstream libraries. The honest read was: Ruby-heavy shop, takes email deliverability seriously, ships small utility patches. Don't inflate that into a proprietary-engineering-primitives story. Understate rather than oversell — it reads more credible and is actually true.

## Anti-pattern: answering the wrong question

"What impressed you about [Company]?" is not asking for company praise. It's asking "why do you want this job, over others." A sane answer talks about:
- Stack / tooling match
- Location / remote / timezone fit
- Seniority / scope / autonomy
- Problem difficulty vs. what else is on the market
- Size of company tradeoffs

A sane answer does NOT talk about:
- Company mission statements
- Marketing copy
- Adjective-laden praise for their engineering
- Anything that could be copy-pasted into another application

## Anti-pattern: over-engineering prose

The site copy went through multiple rounds of being "too curated." The version that finally worked was distilled from Sander talking out loud — not from a writing exercise. When drafting, imagine him saying it at a bar, then clean up the grammar. That's the target.
